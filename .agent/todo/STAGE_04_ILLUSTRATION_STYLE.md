# Stage 4 — Architectural Illustration Style

## 1. Purpose

Transform the structurally corrected SVG into a coherent illustrated architectural diorama.

The geometry established in Stages 1–3.5 is now the foundation.

This stage introduces the visual language of the project:

* refined architectural linework;
* hand-drawn character;
* subtle irregularities;
* sectional/cutaway illustration treatment;
* visual hierarchy;
* technical-illustration qualities.

The result should begin to resemble a sophisticated hand-drawn architectural model rather than a technical wireframe.

The building layout, perspective, floor arrangement, circulation, and career-stage placement should remain unchanged.

---

# 2. References

Use:

* `PROJECT_BRIEF.md` for the overall visual direction and architectural references.
* `IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md` for the stage sequence.
* The current Stage 3.5 SVG/rendered result as the structural source.
* The selected architectural references already defined in the Brief.

The Brief defines the intended visual character; this stage defines how to begin translating that character into the SVG.

Do not redesign the building based on the references.

The references are primarily a **style reference**, not a geometry template.

---

# 3. Main Objective

The current image is intentionally simple and diagrammatic.

The goal of this stage is to make the same architecture feel like:

> a hand-drawn sectional architectural illustration of a physical miniature building.

The viewer should still immediately understand the existing structure, but the drawing should no longer feel like CAD geometry or a generic SVG wireframe.

---

# 4. Preserve the Architecture

Do not substantially change:

* building footprint;
* floor positions;
* floor heights;
* camera angle;
* perspective;
* elevator location;
* staircase location;
* entrance location;
* internal section divisions;
* wall openings;
* experience-tag positions;
* chronological elevator structure.

Stage 4 is primarily a **visual treatment pass**.

Any geometry change must have a clear visual/style justification.

---

# 5. Linework

The most important visual change in this stage is the linework.

Replace the current uniform technical strokes with a more sophisticated architectural drawing system.

### Establish line hierarchy

Not every line should have the same visual weight.

Use different levels for:

### Primary structural lines

Used for:

* major building edges;
* floor slab edges;
* important exterior boundaries;
* major wall intersections.

These should be the strongest lines.

### Secondary architectural lines

Used for:

* partitions;
* elevator details;
* staircase geometry;
* openings;
* secondary structural elements.

These should be visibly lighter than primary structure.

### Tertiary/detail lines

Used later for:

* small architectural details;
* seams;
* construction elements;
* material indications.

These should remain subtle.

Do not introduce large quantities of tertiary detail yet.

---

# 6. Hand-Drawn Character

Introduce subtle imperfections into the otherwise geometric SVG.

The goal is **not** to make the building look distorted.

Instead:

* straight lines may have extremely small natural variations;
* corners should avoid looking mechanically perfect;
* repeated elements may have slight differences;
* line endings can have a subtle drawn quality;
* small architectural elements can have controlled irregularity.

The irregularity must remain restrained.

The building must still feel carefully designed.

Avoid:

* cartoon-like wobbling;
* exaggerated sketch lines;
* random distortion;
* deliberately messy geometry.

The desired effect is:

> precise architectural drawing made by hand,

not:

> inaccurate drawing.

---

# 7. Sectional / Cutaway Treatment

Strengthen the visual distinction between:

* surfaces that are cut;
* surfaces that are behind;
* visible architectural edges;
* open spaces.

The viewer should understand that the building is intentionally sectioned.

The existing open front/right configuration should remain.

Do not close the building simply to make it look more finished.

The absence of the cutaway walls is part of the visual concept.

---

# 8. Architectural Depth

Use line hierarchy and subtle visual differentiation to reinforce depth.

Elements closer to the viewer may have:

* slightly stronger outlines;
* clearer edges.

Elements farther inside the building may have:

* lighter lines;
* reduced contrast;
* less visual emphasis.

Do not create photographic depth.

The effect should remain similar to an architectural illustration or miniature model drawing.

---

# 9. Floor Slabs

Refine the visual treatment of the floor slabs.

The current slabs are structurally correct but visually quite uniform.

Make them read more naturally as architectural floor plates.

Use:

* differentiated top and edge treatment;
* clearer slab boundaries;
* subtle line hierarchy;
* restrained surface indication.

Do not dramatically increase slab thickness.

Do not introduce realistic concrete rendering yet.

---

# 10. Walls

Give the walls a more deliberate architectural presence.

Walls should no longer look like simple polygons bounded by identical lines.

Use:

* stronger structural outlines;
* lighter interior boundaries;
* subtle indication of wall thickness;
* clearer distinction between wall surfaces and open space.

The cutaway walls should remain visually open where required.

---

# 11. Openings

Refine the openings created in Stage 3.5.

They should now look intentional and architectural rather than like missing pieces of geometry.

Maintain the corrected structure:

* no incorrect horizontal line across the top;
* wall surface continues naturally above the opening;
* opening remains visibly connected to the adjacent space.

Use line hierarchy to make the opening readable without adding decorative door frames.

---

# 12. Staircase Illustration

The staircase geometry from Stage 3.5 should remain unchanged unless a very minor adjustment is necessary.

Improve its visual treatment through:

* clearer step outlines;
* appropriate line hierarchy;
* subtle depth indication;
* cleaner landing definition;
* consistent architectural drawing style.

The staircase should feel like part of the building.

Do not make it visually heavier again.

Avoid adding:

* railings;
* decorative elements;
* furniture;
* excessive construction details.

Those can be considered later.

---

# 13. Elevator Illustration

The elevator should become one of the recognizable visual anchors of the building.

Maintain the corrected compact form.

Improve:

* shaft outline;
* door definition;
* vertical continuity;
* relationship with the floor slabs;
* visible wall surfaces;
* chronological markers.

The elevator should no longer read primarily as a glass structure.

It should read as an architectural elevator shaft integrated into the building.

---

# 14. Elevator Timeline

Preserve the chronological information already introduced during Stage 3.5.

The year/period markers should become part of the illustration rather than looking like temporary labels.

They should:

* follow the elevator structure;
* remain visually secondary to the architecture;
* have consistent proportions;
* align vertically;
* feel physically attached to the elevator surface.

Do not turn them into a large UI timeline.

The elevator remains an architectural object first.

---

# 15. Fifth-Floor Elevator

Preserve the open elevator doors on the fifth floor.

Use the illustration treatment to make this opening visually meaningful.

The viewer should be able to perceive:

* the elevator shaft;
* the open doors;
* the unfinished fifth floor beyond it.

Do not add `CURRENT`.

The architecture and chronology should communicate the concept.

---

# 16. Experience Tags

The experience tags introduced during Stage 3.5 should now receive their first real visual treatment.

They should feel like **physical plaques/signs attached to the building**, not UI labels.

Maintain their current spatial positions.

Improve:

* outline;
* typography treatment;
* perspective;
* attachment to the wall;
* visual hierarchy;
* consistency between tags.

They should remain relatively small.

The building itself must remain the dominant visual element.

---

# 17. Tag Material / Physical Appearance

Give the tags a restrained physical quality.

They may have:

* a thin border;
* slight thickness;
* subtle shadow or offset;
* a small mounting indication;
* slightly different treatment from the wall.

The effect should suggest:

> small architectural identification plaques.

Avoid:

* floating text;
* speech bubbles;
* modern UI cards;
* large banners;
* excessive 3D effects.

---

# 18. Entrance Sign

Apply the same architectural treatment to the exterior sign.

The existing physical placement and support should be preserved.

At this stage, improve its visual integration:

* cleaner structure;
* coherent outline;
* appropriate proportions;
* architectural rather than UI-like appearance.

The actual final profile photograph, QR code, and final typography do not need to be implemented yet if doing so would introduce unnecessary information-layer work.

The sign should visually establish the correct object.

---

# 19. Remove Construction / Guide Appearance

The current SVG still contains some elements that read as construction guides rather than finished architectural illustration.

Review:

* dashed perspective/construction lines;
* temporary guide geometry;
* redundant reference lines;
* auxiliary outlines that do not represent architecture.

Remove or visually suppress elements that are only useful during development.

Do not remove any line that is necessary to communicate actual architecture.

The final result of Stage 4 should no longer look like an active construction diagram.

---

# 20. Visual Hierarchy

Establish a clear hierarchy between the different types of information.

Recommended order:

1. building silhouette and major structure;
2. floor architecture;
3. staircase/elevator;
4. major section divisions;
5. experience tags and chronology;
6. secondary architectural details.

Nothing introduced in this stage should compete with the building itself.

---

# 21. Color Direction

Begin introducing the restrained visual palette described in the Brief.

The palette should move away from the completely neutral wireframe while remaining subtle.

Use:

* warm off-white / paper-like backgrounds;
* muted architectural tones;
* restrained gray values;
* desaturated accents where useful.

Avoid highly saturated colors.

Avoid corporate infographic palettes.

Avoid making every floor a different color.

The color system should support the architectural illustration rather than categorizing the career stages through arbitrary colors.

---

# 22. Shading

Introduce only very subtle shading.

Possible uses:

* underside of floor slabs;
* recessed wall areas;
* elevator depth;
* staircase depth;
* architectural openings;
* contact between structural elements.

Shading should remain soft and restrained.

Do not attempt photorealistic lighting.

Do not introduce dramatic shadows.

The drawing should still look illustrated rather than rendered.

---

# 23. Surface Treatment

Very limited surface treatment may be introduced where it improves the architectural illustration.

Possible examples:

* extremely subtle paper-like variation;
* restrained surface grain;
* slight material differentiation;
* very light architectural hatching.

Do not yet build a complete material system.

Detailed materials and architectural micro-details belong to the following stage.

---

# 24. Hatching and Technical Illustration

Introduce restrained architectural hatching where appropriate.

Potential uses:

* cut wall surfaces;
* floor slab edges;
* selected structural areas;
* recessed elements.

Hatching should:

* follow the architectural surface;
* remain subtle;
* reinforce depth;
* never obscure the geometry.

Avoid filling large areas with dense cross-hatching.

The goal is to suggest technical illustration, not produce a blueprint.

---

# 25. Structural Detail

Only introduce structural details that reinforce the new illustration language.

Examples:

* subtle wall thickness;
* slab edge indications;
* simple brackets;
* restrained seams;
* minimal structural joints.

Do not begin adding large quantities of:

* pipes;
* cables;
* HVAC systems;
* furniture;
* machinery;
* decorative objects.

That belongs primarily to Stage 5.

---

# 26. Repetition and Consistency

Repeated architectural elements must share a common visual language.

This applies especially to:

* floor slabs;
* elevator doors;
* experience tags;
* wall edges;
* structural supports;
* staircase steps.

Small hand-drawn variations are allowed, but the elements must still look like members of the same architectural system.

---

# 27. SVG Implementation

Maintain the modular SVG architecture.

Separate visual treatment from structural geometry wherever practical.

For example:

* structural geometry;
* primary outlines;
* secondary outlines;
* shading;
* hatching;
* tags;
* elevator chronology;
* entrance sign.

should remain independently editable.

Avoid converting the illustration into a flattened raster asset.

SVG remains the primary visual source.

---

# 28. No Rasterization

Do not use a generated raster image as the final architectural source.

The illustration must remain:

* vector-based;
* editable;
* modular;
* scalable;
* compatible with later Vue integration.

Raster textures may only be considered later if they can be incorporated without compromising the SVG-first architecture.

---

# 29. Information Layer Boundary

Do not turn Stage 4 into the final information-design stage.

The following remain outside the main scope:

* full descriptions;
* technologies;
* role names;
* years of employment;
* project descriptions;
* detailed career metadata;
* interactive tooltips;
* Detailed/Overview mode.

The current experience labels and elevator chronology are sufficient for this stage.

---

# 30. Minor Geometry Corrections

Minor structural corrections are allowed if they become obvious while applying the illustration style.

Examples:

* a line that should not be visible after applying proper section treatment;
* a tiny intersection artifact;
* a wall edge that requires adjustment to produce a coherent illustrated corner;
* a tag that requires a few pixels of repositioning;
* a small elevator/floor visual collision.

However:

**Do not reopen the major architectural decisions of Stages 1–3.5.**

No new camera redesign.

No new floor layout.

No staircase relocation.

No elevator relocation.

No redesign of the building footprint.

---

# 31. Overall Visual Target

The result should move through the following visual progression:

**Current Stage 3.5**

> clean architectural wireframe / structural diagram

↓

**Stage 4**

> hand-drawn sectional architectural illustration

↓

**Stage 5**

> detailed architectural diorama with materials, textures, objects, and richer environmental detail

Stage 4 should clearly establish the middle step without prematurely attempting Stage 5.

---

# 32. Stage Boundary

Do **not** attempt to fully complete the diorama in this stage.

In particular, defer:

* detailed materials;
* extensive textures;
* furniture;
* company-specific objects;
* project-specific visual metaphors;
* pipes and technical infrastructure;
* environmental details;
* detailed professional content;
* final QR/profile implementation;
* advanced information hierarchy;
* Detailed/Overview modes;
* Vue interaction logic.

These will be addressed in subsequent stages.
