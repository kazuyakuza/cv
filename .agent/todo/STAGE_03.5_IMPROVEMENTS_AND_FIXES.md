# Stage 3.5 — Structural Corrections and Architectural Connections

## 1. Purpose

Resolve the remaining architectural and geometric issues identified after Stage 3, without introducing the final illustration style.

This is a corrective stage.

The building's overall perspective and proportions are already substantially improved in Stage 3 and should be preserved unless a specific correction below requires a local adjustment.

The goal is to make the current building structurally coherent before introducing the visual language defined for Stage 4.

---

## 2. References

Use:

* `PROJECT_BRIEF.md` as the source of truth for the complete building layout, career stages, circulation, orientation, and architectural concept.
* `IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md` for the overall stage sequence.
* The current Stage 3 SVG and rendered output as the starting point.
* This specification for the specific corrections required in this stage.

Do not duplicate career content from the Brief here.

---

## 3. Scope

This stage is intentionally narrow.

### Correct:

* ground-floor openings;
* staircase geometry and placement;
* first-floor floor geometry around the staircase;
* staircase-to-first-floor connection;
* elevator proportions and enclosure;
* elevator visual integration;
* elevator floor indicators;
* final-floor elevator opening;
* remaining structural intersections and occlusion issues.

### Introduce:

* the first version of architectural experience tags/signs attached to the visible walls.

### Do not introduce:

* final illustration style;
* textures;
* materials;
* detailed architectural props;
* detailed company-specific objects;
* final information overlays;
* detailed career descriptions;
* animations or interactions.

Stage 4 will handle the visual transformation after these corrections are complete.

---

# 4. Preserve the Current Composition

The Stage 3 perspective correction should be treated as the new baseline.

Do not redesign the entire building.

Preserve:

* the current overall footprint;
* the current floor proportions;
* the current lower viewing angle;
* the visible floor surfaces;
* the lower-right viewer position;
* the building's upper-left recession;
* the general floor heights;
* the established vertical progression.

Only make global perspective changes if a local correction proves impossible without them.

The purpose of this stage is refinement, not another redesign of the camera.

---

# 5. Spatial Orientation

All corrections must use the established orientation.

When looking at the rendered building:

* **Entrance side:** right/front side.
* **Rear:** toward the left side of the composition.
* **Left wall:** the wall facing toward the viewer/front of the image.
* **Right wall:** the wall receding toward the rear of the image.

This orientation must remain consistent for:

* staircase placement;
* elevator access;
* wall openings;
* experience tags;
* entrance;
* partitions.

---

# 6. Ground-Floor Openings

Correct the existing openings between the ground-floor career sections.

The current openings contain an incorrect horizontal line along the upper portion of the opening.

That line currently reads as if it were:

* a lintel;
* a beam;
* or the top boundary of the opening.

That is not intended.

### Required result

The upper part of the opening must visually read as **the continuation of the wall surface**.

In other words:

* remove the incorrect line;
* do not introduce an artificial frame;
* keep the wall surface visually continuous above the opening;
* preserve the actual void/opening below it.

The opening should therefore read as a simple passage through the wall rather than as a framed doorway.

### Important

Do not add a detailed door.

The goal is simply:

> wall → opening → wall

with no unexplained horizontal structural line across the upper portion.

Apply the same principle to equivalent openings if the same geometry problem exists elsewhere.

---

# 7. Staircase — Position

Refine the ground-floor staircase.

The staircase is already broadly in the correct area, but it currently sits too close to the left wall.

Move it slightly inward.

### Required relationship

There must be a small but clearly visible gap between:

> left building wall ↔ staircase

The gap does not need to represent a fully realistic architectural corridor.

Visual separation is more important than strict physical realism.

The staircase must not appear attached to, fused with, or growing directly out of the wall.

A small consistent spatial gap is sufficient.

---

# 8. Staircase — Scale

Reduce the staircase's overall visual mass.

The current staircase is too large and visually dominant.

Reduce its size so that:

* it occupies less of the floor;
* it does not compete with the career sections;
* it feels proportional to the building;
* it remains clearly readable;
* it does not become visually cramped.

Do not reduce it so much that the individual steps or direction of travel become unclear.

The staircase should feel like a circulation element within the building, not one of its largest architectural volumes.

---

# 9. Staircase — Length and Vertical Reach

Correct the staircase length.

The staircase should have the **minimum appropriate length required to connect the ground floor to the first floor** within the established perspective.

The current version visually appears longer than necessary.

Adjust:

* number/spacing of visible steps if necessary;
* horizontal run;
* vertical rise;
* landing position.

Do not solve the problem by arbitrarily changing the building's floor height.

The staircase must terminate at the correct first-floor elevation.

---

# 10. Staircase — First-Floor Connection

The connection between the staircase and the first floor must be rebuilt as a coherent architectural transition.

The current first-floor geometry around the Nextcode section contains an incorrect floor shape:

* part of the floor appears to accommodate the staircase;
* another part does not;
* the resulting boundary does not read as one coherent floor.

Correct this geometry.

### Required result

The first-floor slab should remain structurally continuous while containing a deliberate staircase access/opening where necessary.

The staircase should:

1. rise from the ground floor;
2. pass beneath/through the first-floor structure as appropriate;
3. reach a clearly defined landing;
4. connect directly to the Nextcode area.

There should be no arbitrary missing piece of floor.

---

# 11. Staircase — Landing

Create a clear landing at the top of the staircase.

The landing should:

* belong to the first floor;
* align with the first-floor elevation;
* connect directly to Nextcode;
* have a visually understandable boundary;
* not appear to float independently of the floor.

The landing does not need detailed railing or furniture.

Its purpose is to make the circulation path unambiguous.

The complete circulation should read as:

> Ground floor → staircase → landing → Nextcode / first floor

---

# 12. Staircase — Occlusion

Review the SVG layer order around the staircase.

The first-floor slab must visually occupy the correct position relative to the staircase.

The result should communicate that:

* the staircase exists below the first-floor slab;
* the slab hides the portion that would physically be behind/inside it;
* the staircase becomes visible again at the landing/opening.

Do not simply place the entire staircase on top of the first-floor geometry.

Likewise, do not hide the entire staircase behind the slab.

The visible result must communicate a physical connection between the two levels.

---

# 13. Elevator — General Form

Refine the elevator so that it reads as an integrated architectural element.

The current elevator is too wide and the large glazed area makes it visually resemble an independent glass tower.

Reduce its visual width.

The elevator should become:

* narrower;
* more compact;
* more integrated with the floor structure;
* visually subordinate to the building as a whole.

It should still be immediately recognizable as the central vertical circulation element.

---

# 14. Elevator — Enclosure

Reduce or remove the excessive transparent/glazed appearance.

The elevator does not need to be represented as a completely transparent shaft.

Introduce a more solid architectural enclosure where appropriate.

The visible portion should provide enough structural mass that the elevator feels attached to the building rather than standing beside it.

The shaft must remain understandable as a continuous vertical element.

Do not completely hide the elevator.

There must remain enough visual evidence to identify:

* the shaft;
* the elevator position;
* the doors;
* the vertical continuity.

---

# 15. Elevator — Access Orientation

Preserve the correction made during Stage 3:

**elevator doors must face the right side of the building.**

Do not revert to the previous orientation.

Each floor's elevator access should be consistent with this orientation.

The elevator shaft itself may be partially enclosed from the viewer's perspective, but the architectural logic of the doors must remain clear.

---

# 16. Elevator — Wall / Timeline Surface

Use the more solid visible elevator wall as an opportunity for a future vertical career timeline.

At this stage, establish only the structural/visual surface.

The wall should be suitable for later displaying:

* years;
* decades;
* or another compact chronological marker.

The exact typography and final information design belong to later stages.

The timeline should conceptually communicate that the elevator travels through the chronology of the career.

This removes the need for a separate `CURRENT` label on the fifth floor.

---

# 17. Elevator — Floor Indicators

Introduce a simple indication of the temporal progression associated with the elevator.

The implementation can initially be minimal.

Possible information:

* year;
* year range;
* decade;
* compact chronological marker.

The exact presentation should remain visually simple at this stage.

Do not turn the elevator into a large text panel.

The future visual hierarchy should remain:

> architectural elevator first → chronological information second.

---

# 18. Fifth-Floor Elevator

The fifth-floor elevator requires a deliberate exception.

At the current/final floor:

* the elevator should remain visibly present;
* its position should be easy to identify;
* the elevator doors should be open;
* the opening should visually connect the elevator with the unfinished fifth-floor environment.

This is the only floor where the elevator should visibly communicate an open/current destination.

The open doors should reinforce the idea that the elevator has reached the current stage of the career.

Do not add a `CURRENT` text label.

The architectural situation should communicate this by itself.

---

# 19. Remaining Elevator/Floor Intersections

Audit every floor where the elevator crosses a slab.

Check for:

* incorrect gaps;
* slab lines passing through the elevator;
* elevator walls incorrectly stopping at floors;
* floating shaft segments;
* incorrect occlusion;
* duplicated outlines.

The elevator should appear to pass continuously through the building while remaining properly integrated with each floor.

# 20. Experience Tags

Introduce the first architectural representation of the career-stage labels.

These are **not HTML information overlays**.

They are small physical-looking signs/plaques attached directly to the visible building walls.

Their purpose is to identify each career section without placing large floating text inside the rooms.

# 21. Tag Orientation

The tags attached to the left-facing wall must follow that wall's plane.

They should appear:

* mounted on the wall;
* parallel to the wall surface;
* integrated into the architectural drawing;
* visually similar to small plaques/signs.

They must not appear as:

* floating labels;
* perpendicular signs projecting into the room;
* detached UI elements.

Their exact visual treatment can remain simple until Stage 4.
